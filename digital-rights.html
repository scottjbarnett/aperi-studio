Digital Rights Infrastructure

Rights that travel with the person.

What would digital rights look like if they were enforceable by tools, agents, and institutions - not just written in policies?

Concept room

The public-facing frame

Aperi Studio explores the future of digital rights: data sovereignty, identity, consent, audit, refusal, portability, global access, and agentic AI.

The central idea is simple: a person should not become a passive user account inside someone else's system. Their rights should travel with them.

This work develops ideas that have previously sat under the internal working name CivicOS. For public-facing work, the clearer frame is digital rights infrastructure.

The missing layer: time-bounded consent

Consent is often treated as a one-time event: a checkbox, a form, a policy agreement, or a vague acceptance of terms.

But real consent changes over time.

A person may consent to data being used for one purpose, by one organisation, for one period, under one set of conditions. That consent may later expire, be withdrawn, be narrowed, be renewed, or become invalid because the context has changed.

Digital rights infrastructure should therefore treat consent as time-bounded, purpose-bounded, and revocable.

Consent should be time-bounded

Consent should have time limits, conditions, purpose, and renewal points. Indefinite consent should be treated as a risk, not a default.

What a consent system should answer

  • What was consented to?
  • Who received that consent?
  • For what purpose?
  • For how long?
  • Under what conditions?
  • What happens when consent expires?
  • Can the person withdraw it?
  • Can the person see how it was used?
  • Can an agent act on the person's behalf?

Time-bounded consent turns privacy from a static policy into a living relationship.

Why agentic AI matters

If people are expected to manage identity, privacy, permissions, data access, platform rules, institutional relationships, and consent across many systems, they will need help.

A trusted personal agent could eventually help a person monitor active consents, warn when consent is too broad, refuse unnecessary data access, renew or withdraw permissions, track where data has gone, request deletion or correction, challenge misuse, keep an audit trail, and negotiate access on the person's behalf.

The aim is not to create another platform that owns the person's identity. The aim is to explore whether AI agents can strengthen human sovereignty rather than replace it.

What this is

Digital Rights Infrastructure is a concept stream exploring how digital rights might become enforceable through personal agents, time-bounded consent, identity layers, audit trails, refusal systems, portability, global access, sovereign data and vault structures, and public-interest AI infrastructure.

What this is not

This is not a proposal for one private platform to own identity, truth, permission, or reputation. It is not a claim that AI agents automatically solve digital rights. The question is more careful: can agentic systems, if properly governed, help people retain agency, memory, consent, and recourse in an increasingly automated world?

Core concepts

The digital citizen is not a user profile

A person is more than the data held about them. Digital rights should begin from the embodied person, not from the platform account.

A person should retain rights over their digital identity, including how it is used, represented, shared, monetised, or used to generate value.

Consent should be time-bounded

Consent should have time limits, conditions, purpose, and renewal points. Indefinite consent should be treated as a risk, not a default.

Refusal is a right

A meaningful consent system must include the ability to say no, withdraw, narrow, or challenge access.

Sovereign data and vault

Systems remember people constantly. The question is whether that memory serves the person, the institution, or the platform.

Agents need governance

Personal agents could help people manage data rights, but they must themselves be accountable, auditable, contestable, and under human control.

Portability and global access

Digital rights should remain meaningful across services, institutions, jurisdictions, and automated systems rather than being trapped inside a single account or platform.
